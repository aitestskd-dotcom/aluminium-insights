Beyond the smelter: three levers to decarbonise aluminium that boards under-use

Operate better, close the loop, and turn red mud from liability into feedstock

Sushant Kumar Dalai · Industrial decarbonisation and circular economy · Dubai · October 2026

Executive summary

  • Three levers sit within operator and buyer control and get less board attention than smelter power: operating efficiency (including AI-enabled process optimisation), circular supply, and bauxite-residue valorisation.
  • Operate better. The IEA estimates that AI-enabled process optimisation could cut energy costs in energy-intensive industries by 3–10 percentage points, yet industrial deployment lags. At EGA, I contributed to programmes that delivered a 15% improvement in energy intensity.
  • Close the loop. Recycled aluminium needs about 95% less energy than primary metal (8.3 versus 186 GJ per tonne). Global beverage-can recycling is 75%, against 58.7% in the Middle East and North Africa and an industry target of 80% by 2030.
  • Turn red mud into feedstock. About 177 million tonnes of residue were generated in 2023, while only 2.5–5 million tonnes a year are reused. The global inventory exceeds 4 billion tonnes and the IAI projects 10 billion by 2050. Early-stage technologies now target aluminium, scandium, titanium and rare earths in it, but none is proven at scale.
  • Sequence the levers: operations first (months), circular supply next (years), residue pilots in parallel (multi-year), each with its own owner and evidence bar.

1. Smelter power is only part of the picture

Primary aluminium production is electricity-intensive, and the International Aluminium Institute (IAI) puts the global footprint of primary metal at 15.1 tCO₂e per tonne (mine to cast house, 2022). That makes power procurement the headline lever, and rightly so. But a board that treats decarbonisation as a power-purchase question leaves three other levers unmanaged: how efficiently existing assets run, how much metal comes from scrap, and what happens to the by-products of refining.

2. Lever 1: operate better

Energy intensity is the least glamorous lever and the fastest. At EGA, I contributed to programmes that delivered a 15% improvement in energy intensity, an 18% improvement in overall equipment effectiveness and a 25% reduction in unplanned downtime, through process monitoring, operating discipline and maintenance coordination.

AI is the new accelerator. The IEA has estimated that AI-enabled process optimisation could reduce energy costs in energy-intensive industries such as cement by 3–10 percentage points, and it notes that industrial deployment lags investment in AI infrastructure. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study published in September 2026 modelled a 3–9% reduction in primary energy demand and CO₂ emissions at a dry-process cement plant, after counting the electricity the AI itself consumes.

Two cautions follow. These ranges come from cement and cross-sector estimates, not from alumina refining, so every plant needs its own baseline. And the gains depend on data quality and operator trust, which is why the first step is instrumentation and a clean baseline, not a model. In my advisory work on AI-enabled manufacturing efficiency, I treat energy and carbon as one measurement problem: the meter data that finds a saving is the same data that evidences it for CBAM and disclosure.

3. Lever 2: close the loop

Recycling is the largest single efficiency in the aluminium system. The IAI compares 186 GJ of primary energy per tonne for primary aluminium (mine to cast house, 2019) with 8.3 GJ for recycled metal, a saving of about 95.5%. In a net-zero-by-2050 scenario, RMI forecasts that recycled metal must rise from about 34% of supply (2018) to 54%.

Used beverage cans (UBCs) are the most visible scrap stream. The latest IAI and Eunomia global dataset, published in November 2025, shows 75% of aluminium beverage cans recycled worldwide, ahead of PET bottles at 47% and glass at 42%. The Middle East and North Africa recorded 58.7%, based on four countries, against 94% in Latin America. The Global Beverage Can Circularity Alliance targets 80% by 2030 and close to 100% by 2050.

The prize is large. An IAI-commissioned Roland Berger study found that effective global UBC recycling could save 60 million tonnes of CO₂e a year by 2030, with can consumption forecast to rise from 420 billion to 630 billion cans between 2020 and 2030. The Gulf and Asia-Pacific are major hubs in the UBC scrap trade.

Recycling is as much a sourcing and quality discipline as an environmental one: traceable origin, consistent specification and reliable logistics decide whether scrap becomes usable feedstock. These are the elements I focused on when building sourcing, quality-assurance and logistics pathways for UBC and secondary-aluminium supply.

4. Lever 3: turn red mud from liability into feedstock

Every tonne of alumina leaves behind roughly 1.2 tonnes of bauxite residue, known as red mud, according to the IAI. About 177 million tonnes were generated in 2023 against 141.8 million tonnes of alumina. The global inventory exceeds 4 billion tonnes, and the IAI projects 10 billion by 2050. Only 2.5–5 million tonnes a year are reused today, mainly in cement, bricks and soil applications, which is under 3% of annual generation at the upper end of that range.

A new generation of technologies targets the metals in this residue. FAST Metals, a US start-up founded in 2024, raised $4.3 million in pre-seed funding in July 2026 for a process that separates aluminium, scandium, titanium and rare earths from red mud, and reports a commercial contract to treat about one tonne of red mud and refinery waste a week. That is a credible start and a long way from scale: at the IAI average ratio, a refinery producing 2 million tonnes of alumina a year generates roughly 2.4 million tonnes of residue annually, a gap of more than four orders of magnitude (illustrative).

The questions that decide whether a pilot proceeds are rarely about chemistry. They are about feedstock variability, permitting, offtake and who carries pilot risk. My own work in this space has covered feedstock evaluation, technology screening, techno-economic inputs and permitting review for bauxite-residue recovery, and facilitating early technical and commercial discussions between a US technology developer and a Middle East alumina refinery.

Five questions for the board

  1. What is our energy intensity per tonne against our own best-demonstrated level, and which unit would we instrument first?
  2. How much of our metal could come from traceable scrap, and what specification gates it?
  3. Where do we stand against the 80% can-recycling target in the markets we serve?
  4. What is our residue inventory, and which pilot partner and permit route would let us test valorisation within 24 months?
  5. Which of these levers do our CBAM data and disclosure systems already evidence?
